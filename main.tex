\begin{abstract}
Hybrid quantum--classical models provide a practical setting for studying parameterized quantum circuits inside otherwise classical learning pipelines. We investigate a specific architectural question in reinforcement learning for portfolio allocation: how does the empirical behaviour of a policy change when one latent block is absent, instantiated classically, or implemented as a variational quantum circuit? Starting from the EI3 policy available in RLPortfolio, we construct a common scaffold and evaluate each configuration over 20 training runs. The quantum configurations are evaluated with exact statevector expectation values. Under the experimental protocol used here, the tested quantum configurations attain higher mean final portfolio values and lower run-to-run dispersion than the tested classical configurations. In the register-width experiment, the reported final-value distributions for 2, 4, and 8 qubits are not separated by the Kruskal--Wallis test ($H(2)=2.13$, $p=0.345$). Because register width and pseudorandom stream are confounded in that sweep, and because the classical and quantum campaigns are not seed-paired, these results are interpreted as an empirical characterization of the tested architectures rather than evidence of quantum advantage or of a uniquely quantum mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantum machine learning (QML) investigates how quantum information processing can be incorporated into learning algorithms, including hybrid approaches in which parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) are optimized together with classical components~\cite{Biamonte2017Quantummachinelear,Benedetti2019Parameterizedquant,Cerezo2021Variationalquantum}. PQCs provide a flexible interface for this setting because classical data can be encoded into a quantum state, processed by a trainable variational circuit, and returned to a classical pipeline through measured expectation values. At the same time, the broader QML literature cautions against interpreting the use of a quantum model, parameter-count reductions, or performance on a small benchmark as evidence of practical quantum advantage. Trainability, data encoding, classical simulability, baseline selection, and evaluation methodology remain central to such comparisons~\cite{Schuld2021Effectofdataencodi,Huang2021Powerofdatainquant,Tang2022Dequantizingalgori,McClean2018Barrenplateausinqu}.

The reported experiments are interpreted as a characterization of these architectures under the specified dataset, training protocol, and statevector-simulation regime. They do not establish quantum computational advantage or identify a uniquely quantum mechanism for any observed difference.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Portfolio optimisation as a reinforcement-learning problem}
\label{subsec:bg-portfolio}

In the formulation of Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2017deepreinforcementlearningframework}, capital allocation is cast as a deterministic policy that maps a window of recent price history to a weight vector on the simplex. The state is a tensor of price-derived features across assets and time; the action is the target allocation; and the reward is the logarithmic growth of portfolio value net of transaction costs, so that the objective optimised by policy gradient is the cumulative log-return of the episode.

The architecture underpinning this formulation is the Ensemble of Identical Independent Evaluators (EIIE). Each asset is scored by a weight-sharing network applied independently along the asset axis, and the scores are normalised into an allocation. Weight sharing has two consequences that matter here: the evaluation of an asset does not depend on its index within the universe, and the network generalises across universes of different cardinality. Two further mechanisms are inherited by all models in this paper --- the feedback of the previous weight vector, which allows the agent to price the cost of rebalancing, and convolutions along the temporal axis, which compress each asset's history into a compact representation. EI3~\cite{RLportfolio} extends EIIE with a three-headed convolutional block that aggregates short-, medium- and long-term temporal structure, and was evaluated on Brazilian equities, which makes it the natural baseline for the present testbed.

\subsection{Parameterised quantum circuits as trainable layers}
\label{subsec:bg-pqc}

The quantum component studied in this work is a parameterised quantum circuit (PQC) used as a trainable latent layer. A PQC layer requires three ingredients that are directly relevant to the architecture developed below: a map from classical features to a quantum state, a parameterised unitary, and a measurement that returns classical values to the surrounding network~\cite{Benedetti2019Parameterizedquant,Cerezo2021Variationalquantum}.

\sam{\textbf{[Use $n$ for the qubit dimension]} Given a latent dimension $n$, the classical network first outputs a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$. In QEI3, the entries of this vector are then mapped to an $n$-qubit register by applying single-qubit $R_Y(x_i)$ rotations on each qubit. The encoded state is processed by a RealAmplitudes variational circuit composed of trainable $R_Y$ rotations and full entanglement. The circuit is then read out through the local expectation values $\langle Z_i\rangle$, producing a $n$-dimensional real vector that can be consumed by the classical decision head.}

This input--output structure is central to the comparison in this paper. Each local Pauli-$Z$ expectation value lies in $[-1,1]$, so the quantum block has a bounded $n$-dimensional output. The classical counterpart is therefore constructed with the same latent width and a bounded $\tanh$ output, reducing differences in the interfaces presented to the downstream network. Matching input and output dimensions does not make the classical and quantum blocks equivalent in expressive capacity; it provides a common architectural location at which their empirical behavior can be compared.

\sam{ Across all experiments, we employ the RealAmplitudes ansatz with full entanglement and a single repetition. This yields $2n$ trainable parameters for an $n$-qubit circuit, while the number of CNOT gates scales as $\binom{n}{2}$. We emphasize that we make no claim that this ansatz or entanglement pattern is optimal for the portfolio task.}

\section{Experimental testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

\subsection{Environment and dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset}

All experiments use the RLPortfolio framework~\cite{RLportfolio}, which provides a Gym-compatible environment for portfolio allocation together with the policy-gradient training loop and the online-learning mechanism used during evaluation.

The universe consists of ten equities traded on the Brazilian exchange (B3): ABEV3, BBAS3, BBDC4, ITUB4, LREN3, PETR4, PRIO3, RENT3, VALE3 and WEGE3, with daily closing, high and low prices retrieved from Yahoo Finance. Prices are normalised per asset with a maximum-absolute scaler fitted exclusively on the training window and applied unchanged to the test window; in addition, each observation window is normalised inside the environment by its last closing price. A single observation is therefore a tensor of shape $3 \times 10 \times 50$ (features $\times$ assets $\times$ time window), the window of \num{50} trading days being the history available to the agent at each decision step.

The split is out-of-time: training spans 2017-01-01 to 2019-12-31 and testing spans 2020-01-01 to 2020-12-31, the latter yielding \num{198} decision steps (\num{199} portfolio-value observations including the initial state). Every simulation starts from an initial capital of \num{100000} monetary units and pays a proportional transaction cost of \SI{0.25}{\percent} on rebalancing. The output layer emits eleven weights: the ten assets plus a cash position representing the option to remain liquid and unremunerated.

Two properties of this testbed should be kept in view when reading the results. The test window is a single year, and an atypical one, containing both a sharp drawdown and a strong recovery; the trajectories in \ref{sec:results} therefore describe behaviour under one realised market path, not an expectation over market regimes. And no held-out validation partition is used: the test environment also serves for periodic validation, and online learning is enabled during the test episode. Both choices are inherited from the reference framework and are retained so that all models --- baseline, classical and hybrid --- are evaluated under identical conditions; their consequences for interpretation are discussed in \ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Training protocol and statistical treatment}
\label{subsec:protocol}

Because the variability of RL training across initialisations is of the same order as the effects under study, no configuration is characterised by a single run. Each configuration is trained \num{20} times independently, drawing from a single pseudorandom stream seeded at the start of the batch and not reinitialised between runs, so that each run begins from a distinct initialisation and the batch as a whole is reproducible by re-executing the procedure. Because architectures with different parameter counts consume the pseudorandom stream differently, initialisations are \emph{not} paired across models, and all comparisons between configurations are treated as independent samples.

The quantity reported throughout is the portfolio value along the test window, summarised at the last step as the \emph{final value}. Figures show the mean trajectory with a $\pm 1$ standard deviation envelope across runs. Dispersion is compared through the coefficient of variation, which is the appropriate scale-free measure here because the configurations differ in mean level.

Two sets of training hyperparameters are used (\ref{tab:hyperparams}). The \emph{unified protocol} applies an identical learning rate, episode count and batch size to every architecture, and is the protocol under which all proposed variants are evaluated; differences between them are therefore attributable to architecture. The \emph{original configuration} reproduces the hyperparameters published for the reference model~\cite{RLportfolio}, and is used to characterise the baseline in its best known conditions, so that the comparison in \ref{sec:results} is made against the strongest available version of EI3 rather than against a weakened one.

\section{From EI3 to a hybridisable architecture}
\label{sec:architectures}

This section describes the four architectures evaluated and, more importantly, the reasoning that connects them. They form a chain in which each step changes exactly one thing relative to the previous one, and it is that chain --- rather than any single model --- that carries the argument of the paper.

\subsection{Baseline: EI3}
\label{subsec:arch-ei3}

The baseline is EI3 as reproduced from the reference implementation~\cite{RLportfolio,RLportfolio} and shown in \ref{fig:arch-ei3}.

The property that makes EI3 a suitable point of departure is precisely the one that makes it unsuitable as a host: the $1\times1$ convolution maps features to allocation scores in a single step, so the network has no intermediate representation whose width is a free parameter. There is no site at which a block of $Q$ neurons --- or $Q$ qubits --- could be inserted without changing the meaning of the surrounding layers.

\subsection{Design constraints imposed by a quantum latent block}
\label{subsec:design-constraints}

Creating such a site requires three changes, each dictated by a property of the quantum block rather than by a search for accuracy.

\paragraph{A latent projection with bounded angles.}
A dense projection maps the eleven convolutional channels to $Q$ values, followed by a $\pi\tanh(\cdot)$ scaling that constrains them to $[-\pi,\pi]$. Because the subsequent angle encoding is periodic, this transformation is used to bound the values presented to the quantum block. It does not make the learned representation injective.

\paragraph{A decision head that consumes the block output.}
Because the block emits $Q$ values in $[-1,1]$ rather than eleven allocation scores, a small MLP head is required to map the latent code back to the simplex. This head is identical in every variant.

\paragraph{Removal of the gated residual branch.}
An early version of the hybrid model included a residual projection from the latent representation around the block, combined with its output through a learnable gate. This is standard practice for trainability, and it was removed deliberately. With a bypass in place, the decision head can be supplied with the pre-block representation directly, and the gate is free to attenuate the block towards irrelevance; the network can then reach good performance while making essentially no use of the layer under study. Any comparison between a quantum and a classical instantiation of that layer would then be uninformative, because neither model is required to use it. Removing the branch forces the decision head to consume the block output and nothing else, which is what makes the ablation identifiable. The cost --- a harder optimisation problem, and the block becoming a genuine information bottleneck --- is accepted for that reason.

\subsection{Classical instantiation: QEI3-eq}
\label{subsec:arch-classical}

QEI3-eq (\ref{fig:arch-classical}) instantiates the latent block classically, as a dense layer of width $Q$ with $\tanh$ activation. The activation is chosen to match the range of the quantum readout: since $\langle Z_i \rangle \in [-1,1]$ by construction, a classical surrogate with unbounded output would differ from the quantum block in codomain as well as in parameterisation, and the ablation would no longer isolate a single factor. At the reference width of eight, the block contributes \num{72} trainable parameters --- a $8 \times 8$ weight matrix and eight biases --- against roughly $5\times10^{3}$ in the surrounding network.

\subsection{Implementation and simulation regime}
\label{subsec:implementation}

Circuits are built and simulated with Qiskit~\cite{qiskit2024} and integrated with the PyTorch network through the TorchConnector interface, so that the classical and quantum components are optimized end-to-end. Quantum expectation values are evaluated exactly with StatevectorEstimator. Gradients with respect to circuit parameters are obtained with SPSAEstimatorGradient~\cite{spall1992}, using a pseudorandom generator distinct from the training stream, and model parameters are updated jointly with AdamW.

Exact statevector expectation values are used throughout the quantum experiments reported in this paper. This choice defines the simulation regime of the study and allows the architectural comparison to be made without introducing hardware effects. The resulting experiments therefore characterize the implemented hybrid model under ideal statevector simulation and should not be interpreted as evidence about execution on noisy quantum hardware.

\section{Limitations and interpretation}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results characterize the architectures and training campaigns evaluated in this study.
\sam{REFERENCE/TEXT PROPOSAL P0 -- Suggested Discussion paragraph: ``The observed separation between the tested classical and quantum campaigns should be interpreted in the context of both positive QRL demonstrations~\cite{Jerbi2021ParametrizedQuantumP,Skolik2022QuantumagentsintheGy,Sequeira2023Policygradientsusing} and recent benchmarking work emphasizing the sensitivity of QRL conclusions to experimental design~\cite{Meyer2025BenchmarkingQuantumR,Kruse2025BenchmarkingQuantumRL}. In portfolio management specifically, the results should also be contrasted with recent QRL work~\cite{Gurgul2026QRLPortfolio} and with evidence that classical DRL performance can be highly sensitive to benchmark choice and transaction costs~\cite{Kruthof2025CanDRLBeat1N}.''}
\sam{REVIEW P1 -- Consider adding a short Discussion before Limitations: directly observed evidence; unresolved mechanism; relation to QRL benchmarking (Meyer et al. 2025; Kruse et al. 2025); relation to dynamic portfolio QRL (Gurgul, Chen and Lessmann 2026, preprint); critical DRL portfolio evidence (Kruthof and Mueller 2025); and next experiments with matched seeds and multiple market windows.} They do not establish quantum computational advantage or identify a uniquely quantum mechanism for the observed differences. The quantum circuits are evaluated with exact statevector expectation values, the evaluation covers a single 2020 market path, and the classical and quantum campaigns are not seed-paired. In the register-width sweep, the pseudorandom seed changes with $Q$, so register width and initialization stream are confounded. These factors limit causal interpretation and generalization beyond the reported testbed.

The principal classical--quantum comparison should therefore be read descriptively. Under the reported protocol, the tested quantum configurations have higher observed mean final portfolio values and lower run-to-run dispersion than the tested classical configurations. Establishing whether these differences are reproducible across market periods and attributable to the latent-block parameterization requires additional controlled experiments, including matched random streams and predefined between-model statistical tests.
